# ASD-STE100 for LLM output — research report

Date: 2026-10-04 · Mode: standard · sources_reviewed: 17 · confidence: medium-high

## Executive summary

ASD-STE100 ("Simplified Technical English") is a controlled natural language
owned by ASD, current Issue 9 (2025-01-15): 53 writing rules in 9 sections plus
a ~900-word controlled dictionary [1][2]. Interest in it for LLMs is recent and
tied to one viral post by Andrej Karpathy (2026-10-02) [6], who asks models to
*explain* in STE because the constraints make output "a lot more readable", and
softens the request to "80% of the way to ASD-STE100" because the spec is
"quite stringent" [6][7].

Two corrections matter before adoption:

- Karpathy's claim is about **readability of model output**, not about writing
  prompts or specs in STE [6]. The "use STE for prompts/agent instructions"
  framing is community-driven, not his [7][17].
- There is **no measured token saving**. The leading self-benchmark explicitly
  found output length unchanged; the only quantified wins are rule-compliance
  scores the skill authors scored with their own linters [9].

Practical recommendation for a lean suite: adopt a **curated subset (~15–20
rules), enforce mechanically where cheap, and never claim ASD compliance**.
Choose rules, not a percentage — a percentage is an opinion, "no sentence over
20 words" is a test [9][10].

## 1. What ASD-STE100 is

| Fact | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Owner / origin | ASD (AeroSpace and Defence Industries Association of Europe); developed from ~1970s, first released 1986 as AECMA Simplified English; renamed ASD-STE100 in 2005 | [1] |
| Current version | Issue 9, 2025-01-15; Issue 10 planned 2028 | [1][3] |
| Structure | Part 1 = 53 writing rules in 9 sections; Part 2 = controlled dictionary | [2] |
| Dictionary | ~900 approved words (one meaning, one part of speech each) + ~1200 non-approved words with alternatives; UPPERCASE = approved, lowercase = not | [1][2] |
| Numeric limits | ≤20 words/procedural sentence (≤25 in notes), ≤25/descriptive sentence, ≤6 sentences/paragraph, noun clusters ≤3 words | [2] |
| Grammar discipline | one word = one meaning; approved part of speech only; active voice (passive only for unknown agent in descriptive text); -ing only as technical noun/modifier; permitted tenses = infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past, simple future, past participle as adjective | [2][3] |
| Access / license | free PDF on request; © ASD, trademark; no reproduction "in whole or in part" without authority; irrevocable free-use rights only for listed industry/government/academic bodies | [2][3] |
| Partial adoption | no formal partial mode; STE "cannot be used alone" — company/project directives set scope | [2][3] |

## 2. Karpathy's actual position

Verbatim, X @karpathy, 2026-10-02 [6]:

> "Something I've had success with: Ask your LLM to explain something in
> ASD-STE100, it's a controlled language specification originally developed for
> aerospace maintenance documentation. LLMs well-versed in this language and it
> comes with heavy constraints on clean writing style that I often find a lot
> more readable. Sometimes I've tried to soften it a bit e.g. ask for '80% of
> the way to ASD-STE100' because the spec is quite stringent."

Context [6]: STE is the first rung of a four-format "ladder" for *understanding
model output* — (1) STE, (2) diagrams/images, (3) HTML pages, (4) 3b1b-style
explainer videos.

- **Do not attribute** an "LLMs read at a certain level" theory to him — no such
  primary statement was found (unverified) [6].
- Independent secondary confirmation exists [7][8].
- Expert review found even Karpathy's *cheat-sheet* misstates STE dictionary
  nuance (e.g. swapping `approximately`/`about`) [7] — a caution against
  treating any prompt-only rule list as authoritative.

## 3. Evidence on benefits and costs

| Claim | Evidence | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fewer linter violations | agent-ste: −95.5% violations vs no-skill; violations/100 words 2.23→0.09 | [9] | med-low (self-reported, own linter) |
| Token/length reduction | **Not found**; benchmark says output length "stayed comparable" | [9] | — |
| Better human comprehension | 1996 study: improved comprehension for aircraft technicians (human, not LLM) | [7] | medium (secondary) |
| Risk of fact loss | Informal test: strict STE prompt → ~47% fewer scored facts mentioned | [7] | low (informal) |
| Nuance / readability cost | "would score horrendously in a college essay"; deliberately flat | [17] | low (opinion) |
| Full compliance impractical | 7/53 rules need human judgement; no tool certifies compliance; experts misstate it | [10][7] | high |
| Model drift | models ignore/degrade without a linter or commit hook | [17][7] | low (anecdote) |
| Owner's warning | ASD/STEMG (Jun 2026 white paper): AI text can *appear* STE-compliant; "plausibility must not be confused with verified compliance"; ASD endorses no tool | [4][5] | high |

Bottom line: a prompt produces "STE-flavored" text, not compliant STE [10].
The evidence base for LLM benefit is thin and self-reported [7].

## 4. The ~20 highest-leverage rules (curated subset)

Synthesis from official FAQ + third-party paraphrases [3][12][13]. This is our
proposed "80%" surface — chosen for checkability, not full coverage.

1. Use only approved words, each in one approved meaning and part of speech.
2. Do not rotate synonyms for the same item.
3. Use a verb for an action, not a nominalization ("install", not "installation of").
4. Avoid phrasal verbs; use the single verb ("remove", not "take off").
5. Permitted tenses: infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past,
   simple future; past participle only as adjective.
6. No present/past perfect ("have received" → "received").
7. Do not use -ing forms as running verbs.
8. Active voice; passive only in descriptive text when the actor is unknown.
9. Procedures use the imperative.
10. One instruction per sentence.
11. ≤20 words per procedural sentence; ≤25 per descriptive sentence.
12. Do not drop subjects, verbs, or articles to shorten.
13. Noun clusters ≤3 words.
14. ≤6 sentences per paragraph; one topic per paragraph.
15. No semicolons.
16. Put a condition before the action it applies to.
17. Put WARNING/CAUTION before the step it applies to.
18. Use vertical lists for sequences/conditions.
19. Use articles consistently; American English spelling.
20. Do not stretch technical nouns/verbs ad hoc.

Caveat baked in (from [7][9][11]): preserve conditions, exceptions, and
modality ("may" ≠ "will"); never trade correctness for brevity.

## 6. License warnings

- The 53 rules and ~900-word dictionary are © ASD and trademarked. Do **not**
  embed or redistribute the dictionary in this repo [2][3].
- A short, independently-worded checklist of principles is defensible; copying
  the standard verbatim is not [12][14].
- Do not label anything "ASD-approved" — ASD certifies no tools [4].

## 7. Implications for sdlc-lean

- Best fit: a **small, independently-worded STE-lite skill** (or a section in
  `communicating-concisely`) applied to human-facing prose and generated docs —
  diagram/table-first still wins over STE when structure is the message.
- Enforce only mechanical rules cheaply (sentence length, semicolons, perfect
  tense, -ing verbs) via the existing `scripts/validate-skills.mjs` pattern;
  leave choice-of-word and meaning to judgement.
- Never claim compliance; state "STE-informed (subset)".
- Measure before/after on our own eval; do not repeat the unverified token
  claims.

## Open questions / conflicts

- No peer-reviewed measure of LLM comprehension, instruction-following, token
  reduction, or cross-model consistency from STE was found.
- Two "2026 preprints" cited by [7] are unnamed/unverified.
- ASD white paper and Issue 9 PDF could not be parsed directly (PDF-only).
